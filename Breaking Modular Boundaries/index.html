Introduction

Mental illness does not discriminate based on socioeconomic status, yet existing treatments do not provide relief for everyone and may cause difficult side effects. This project examines the renewed interest in psychedelic-assisted therapy (PAT), with a focus on DMT and the possibility of using it in carefully supervised clinical settings. DMT's potential is still being researched; it is not an established treatment for mental illness (Barker, 2018).

Research

PAT research investigates whether a small number of supervised psychedelic sessions, combined with therapeutic support, may help people with difficult-to-treat conditions. Clinical trials cited in this project include studies of psilocybin for depression and anxiety; these findings provide context for PAT but do not establish that DMT is effective (Goodwin et al., 2022; Griffiths et al., 2016). DMT-specific clinical benefits and risks require further study.

Mechanisms

DMT acts in part through the serotonin 5-HT2A receptor and can alter communication between brain regions, including activity in networks associated with self-focused thought (Timmermann et al., 2023). Researchers propose that experiences such as ego dissolution and models such as REBUS may help explain changes in thought patterns, while research on neural plasticity explores possible longer-term effects (Carhart-Harris & Friston, 2019; Ly et al., 2018). These are active research areas, not settled explanations of how healing occurs.

Conclusion

Psychedelic-assisted therapy is a promising area of research, but it also raises safety and evidence questions. Acute psychological and physical effects can be intense, and psychedelic studies can be difficult to blind, so participant expectations may affect reported outcomes. More carefully controlled research is needed to determine who may benefit, who may face greater risks, and whether DMT can provide lasting clinical benefit.
